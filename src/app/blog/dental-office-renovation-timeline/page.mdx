# How Long Does It Take to Renovate a Dental Office?

Most dental office renovations in BC run 4 to 7 months from design to opening day, though that range stretches depending on permitting, equipment lead times, and whether you stay open during construction. Setting realistic phase-by-phase expectations upfront is what actually keeps a practice's revenue disruption manageable.

> **Key takeaway.** A typical dental office renovation in BC takes **4 to 7 months** from design through final inspection, across roughly six phases: design and permitting (6–12 weeks), demolition (1–2 weeks), rough-in (3–5 weeks), buildout (4–8 weeks), equipment install (2–4 weeks, often overlapping buildout), and inspection (1–2 weeks). Renovations phased around an active practice typically run **6 to 9 months**.

## Renovation Timeline by Phase

This reflects a mid-size renovation — roughly 3–5 operatories, moderate scope — done as a single continuous project rather than staged around patient hours.

| Phase | Typical duration | What happens |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Design & permitting | 6–12 weeks | Drawings, engineering, municipal permit review and approval |
| Demolition | 1–2 weeks | Removing existing walls, fixtures, flooring |
| Rough-in (plumbing, electrical, HVAC) | 3–5 weeks | New operatory lines, vacuum and compressed air, HVAC modifications |
| Buildout (framing, drywall, flooring, millwork) | 4–8 weeks | Walls closed in, finishes installed, casework fitted |
| Equipment install | 2–4 weeks | Chairs, sterilization units, imaging — often overlaps final buildout |
| Inspection & final walkthrough | 1–2 weeks | Building, plumbing, electrical and fire inspections; occupancy sign-off |
| **Total** | **17–33 weeks (about 4–7.5 months)** | |

## Where the Time Actually Goes

A common misconception is that construction is the slow part. Design and permitting is usually the single longest phase, and the one clients have least visibility into. Municipal review varies significantly across the Lower Mainland — some municipalities turn around a straightforward tenant improvement permit in 4–6 weeks, while anything touching structural or mechanical systems in an occupied building can take 10–12 weeks or longer.

Building permitting into your plan from day one, rather than treating it as a formality, is the difference between a realistic schedule and one that blows past its target opening date.

## Scope Changes the Timeline

Not every project needs the full six-phase timeline. It is worth distinguishing a lighter remodel from a full renovation:

1. **Cosmetic remodel** — new flooring, paint, reception refresh, no plumbing or electrical changes: **3–6 weeks**, often no permit required.
2. **Partial renovation** — reconfiguring one or two operatories, updating finishes, minor mechanical changes: **8–14 weeks** including a shorter permit cycle.
3. **Full renovation** — relocating operatories, adding chairs, major mechanical and electrical rework: **17–33 weeks**, as in the table above.
4. **Full renovation with structural changes** — moving load-bearing walls, expanding into an adjacent unit: add **4–8 weeks** for additional engineering and permitting.

If you are unsure which category your project falls into, that is exactly the scoping conversation worth having before you set a target date with your team or landlord. ## What Extends a Renovation

Three factors consistently push timelines past the typical range:

1. **Permit delays.** Incomplete drawing packages, missing engineering, or a municipality with a backlog can add 4–8 weeks before construction even starts. Submitting a complete, coordinated package the first time is the single biggest lever for staying on schedule.
2. **Equipment lead times.** Chairs, imaging systems and specialized sterilization equipment often carry 8–16 week lead times. Ordered late, equipment becomes the bottleneck at the end of the project rather than construction.
3. **Working around an active practice.** Phasing so you can keep seeing patients — nights, weekends, or one section at a time — protects revenue but typically extends total duration by 30–50% against a full closure.

## Renovating While Staying Open

Many BC practices cannot afford to close entirely for months, so phased renovation is common. Phased projects typically take 6 to 9 months against 4 to 7 for a full closure, because work is sequenced around patient hours, dust and noise containment becomes more involved, and some tasks simply cannot run while patients are in the building.

The trade is real either way. A full closure is faster and cheaper per week but costs you revenue outright; phasing protects revenue and costs you time. Which one is right depends on your patient volume and the size of the scope, and it is worth deciding deliberately rather than by default.

## Conclusion

A dental office renovation in BC realistically runs 4 to 7 months as a continuous project, or 6 to 9 phased around an operating practice. The phase that decides whether you hit that window is the one before anything visible happens: design and permitting. Practices that treat it as real work, submit a complete package, and order long-lead equipment early are the ones that open when they said they would.
