# Optometry & Eye Clinic Construction: What to Know Before You Build

Optometry clinic construction sits in an unusual place: half of the space is a licensed clinical environment, and the other half is a retail store. In BC an eye clinic build-out generally runs $150–$260 per sq ft turnkey, the same band as general medical clinic construction, but the way that budget is distributed looks nothing like a family practice. Exam lanes need controlled lighting and precise sightlines. The optical dispensary needs retail lighting, frame display millwork and security. And the diagnostic equipment in between carries requirements that arrive from the vendor rather than the architect. This guide covers what to resolve before drawings are stamped.

> **Key takeaway.** Budget **$150–$260 per sq ft** for an optometry clinic build in BC — roughly $225,000–$390,000 for a 1,500 sq ft practice, $300,000–$520,000 for a 2,000 sq ft practice, and $450,000–$780,000 for a 3,000 sq ft practice, construction only. The two decisions that shape the floor plan are your refraction method, which sets exam lane length, and how much floor you give the optical dispensary.

They cover the tenant improvement — framing, mechanical, electrical, plumbing, millwork, finishes and inspections — and exclude ophthalmic equipment, frame inventory, furniture and IT.

| Large — 3,000 sq ft | 4+ lanes, contact lens room, large retail floor | $450,000 – $780,000 | 4–6 months |

## The Exam Lane Question That Decides Your Floor Plan

Traditional refraction is calibrated to a 20 foot optical distance between the patient and the chart. You get there one of two ways: a room genuinely 20 feet long, or a mirror system that folds the distance into a room of roughly 10 to 12 feet. Almost every modern clinic uses mirrors, and that single choice is what allows three or four exam lanes to fit into a standard commercial tenancy depth.

This has to be settled with your equipment supplier before the layout is frozen, because it changes everything downstream: how many lanes fit, where the corridor runs, how the pre-test room relates to the lanes, and whether the dispensary gets the storefront glazing or the exam rooms do. Changing refraction method after framing means moving walls.

Two related items that are easy to miss at design:

- **Lane lighting has to be controllable and dimmable to near darkness**, on a switching layout the optometrist can reach from the chair position.
- **Windows in an exam lane are a problem, not a feature.** Daylight is difficult to control for refraction and imaging. Give the glazing to reception and the dispensary.

## The Dispensary Is a Retail Build Inside a Clinical Build

The optical dispensary is where an optometry build stops resembling a medical clinic. It needs:

- **Retail-quality lighting** with good colour rendering, because patients are choosing frames against their own skin tone in your light.
- **Frame display millwork** sized to your inventory model, with the security you need for high-value inventory near a storefront.
- **Mirror placement and adjustment stations** with adequate space and, again, good lighting.
- **An edging or finishing lab** if you are cutting lenses on site, which needs power, dust extraction, a sink and a floor that tolerates equipment vibration.

Owners consistently underestimate this scope because it is not clinical and does not feel like construction. It is millwork and lighting, and it is often the most expensive room in the practice per square foot.

## Equipment Coordination Is the Real Schedule Risk

Ophthalmic diagnostic equipment brings requirements that appear nowhere on an architectural drawing until someone asks the vendor for them:

| Equipment | What construction has to provide |
| --- | --- |
| Autorefractor and pre-test units | Dedicated power, bench depth, heat load allowance |
| OCT and retinal imaging | Stable floor, controlled ambient light, clearance for patient positioning |
| Visual field analyzer | A room that can be darkened, low acoustic distraction |
| Phoropter and chair-stand units | Floor or wall power and data at exact lane positions |
| Edging lab equipment | Power, dust extraction, sink, vibration-tolerant floor |
| Fundus camera / slit lamp | Bench or table power at fixed positions |

Two rules keep this from becoming a change order. First, get equipment cut sheets to the mechanical and electrical consultants before permit drawings, not after. Second, ask for lead times early — some diagnostic units are ordered months ahead, and a lane that is finished but waiting on equipment is not a lane you can bill from.

An optometry clinic costs less than a dental clinic because there is no per-chair water, drain, vacuum and compressed air run. It costs more than an office because the lanes, the lighting control and the equipment coordination are all real scope.

## What to Verify Before Signing the Lease

1. **Depth of the tenancy.** Can it accommodate your lane layout with the refraction method you intend to use?
2. **Electrical capacity.** Pre-test equipment, imaging and an edging lab add up faster than owners expect.
3. **Ceiling height and structure.** Lighting control and mechanical distribution both need room above the ceiling.
4. **Storefront and signage rights.** The dispensary is a retail business and visibility has real revenue attached.
5. **What the landlord's allowance covers.** Our guide to [tenant improvement allowances](/blog/tenant-improvement-allowance-explained/) explains where that boundary usually falls, and the [12-point clinic lease checklist](/blog/commercial-lease-for-a-clinic-checklist/) covers what else to confirm before you commit.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**How much does optometry clinic construction cost in BC?** An optometry clinic build in BC generally runs $150–$260 per sq ft turnkey, the same band as medical clinic construction. A 2,000 sq ft practice therefore costs roughly $300,000–$520,000 for the tenant improvement, before ophthalmic equipment, frame displays, furniture and IT. Optical dispensaries push toward the top of the band because of the millwork and lighting the retail floor needs.

**What makes an eye clinic build different from a general medical clinic?** Three things: pre-test and exam lanes need controlled lighting and, for some refraction methods, a specific sightline distance; the optical dispensary is a retail environment with its own lighting, millwork and security requirements; and diagnostic equipment such as OCT and visual field units carries vibration, clearance and power requirements that have to be coordinated with the vendor before framing.

**How long is a refraction lane and does it affect my floor plan?** Traditional refraction is calibrated to a 20 foot optical distance, achieved either with a room of that length or with a mirror system in a shorter room. Mirror systems allow lanes of roughly 10 to 12 feet, which is why most modern optometry clinics can fit multiple exam lanes into a standard commercial depth. Confirm the method with your equipment supplier before the layout is set.

**Does an optometry clinic need special ventilation?** Less than a dental or surgical space, but more than an office. Exam lanes need comfortable, quiet air distribution that does not blow across a patient's eyes during testing, and any contact lens or minor procedure area needs cleanable surfaces and appropriate hand hygiene placement under CSA Z8000 principles. A pre-test room full of equipment also generates heat that the base building has to carry.

**How long does it take to build an optometry clinic?** Most optometry clinic build-outs in BC run 3 to 5 months of construction once permits are issued, with permitting ahead of that. Equipment lead times are the schedule risk most often missed — some diagnostic units are ordered months in advance, and their rough-in requirements have to be known long before they arrive.

**Can an optometry practice stay open during a renovation?** Often yes, if the clinic has enough lanes to give one up at a time. Phasing an optometry renovation is easier than phasing a dental clinic because the exam lanes are relatively self-contained, but the dispensary and reception are shared and are usually the hardest phase to work around.

## Conclusion

Optometry clinic construction rewards early decisions more than most build types, because two of them — refraction method and dispensary size — determine the floor plan and cannot be changed cheaply once framing is up. Get those settled with your equipment supplier before the drawings go for permit, budget within the $150–$260 per sq ft band for the construction itself, and treat equipment lead times as part of the schedule rather than a detail.
