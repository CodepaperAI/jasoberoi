# Pharmacy Build-Out Timeline in BC: From Lease to Licensed Dispensary

Most pharmacy owners plan the construction and are surprised by everything around it. The build itself is the shortest predictable phase of the project — three to four months for a typical community pharmacy — and it sits inside a much longer sequence of drawings, permits and licensing that determines when you can actually open the door. This guide lays out the whole run from lease to first prescription, phase by phase, and marks the two places where a schedule usually slips.

> **Key takeaway.** Allow **8 to 14 months** from lease signing to a licensed, dispensing pharmacy in BC. Roughly **3 to 4 months** of that is construction for a 1,500 sq ft space. The rest is design, municipal permitting and College of Pharmacists of BC licensing — and the licensing has to run alongside the build, not after it, because the College inspects a finished premises.

## The Pharmacy Build Timeline at a Glance

| Phase | Typical duration | What has to finish before the next phase |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Site selection and lease review | 1–3 months | Base-building capacity confirmed, use permitted under zoning |
| Design and construction drawings | 6–10 weeks | Dispensary layout frozen, millwork drawn |
| Building and trade permits | Varies by municipality | Complete application accepted, permit issued |
| Construction | 3–4 months | Inspections passed, deficiencies closed |
| Licensing and pre-opening inspection | Runs in parallel, closes last | College of Pharmacists of BC approval |
| Fixturing, inventory and PharmaNet setup | 2–4 weeks | Systems live, stock on shelves |

The phases overlap. Read the table as a critical path rather than as a queue — the owners who open on time are the ones who started the licensing and the millwork drawings during design rather than after construction.

## Phase 1 — Site Selection and Lease Review

The lease decides more about your schedule than any later choice. Before signing, confirm the use is permitted under the municipality's zoning, and confirm what the base building actually provides: electrical capacity for refrigeration and dispensing technology, HVAC that can carry the equipment heat, plumbing and drainage near where you need sinks, and a secure delivery path that does not run through a public area.

A space that has been a pharmacy before is worth more than it looks, and a space that has been a restaurant is worth less. Our [12-point clinic lease checklist](/blog/commercial-lease-for-a-clinic-checklist/) covers the buildout issues that cost the most when they surface after signing.

## Phase 2 — Design and Construction Drawings

Six to ten weeks, and it is the phase with the most leverage on everything downstream. Three decisions have to be settled here and are expensive to revisit:

- **The dispensary footprint and workflow** — intake, filling, verification, counselling and pickup, with the sightlines a pharmacist needs across all of them.
- **Security and controlled storage**, which affects walls, doors, hardware and where the safe lands structurally.
- **Whether compounding is in scope**, because a compounding room carries its own ventilation, finish and separation requirements and cannot be added later without redoing the mechanical design.

Dispensary millwork should be drawn during this phase, not after permits. It is fabricated to drawing rather than bought off a shelf, and its lead time is the most common critical path on a pharmacy build.

## Phase 3 — Permits and Approvals

A pharmacy fit-out needs a municipal building permit, the associated trade permits, and Technical Safety BC permits for gas and certain electrical work. What that takes depends entirely on where you are building, and most Lower Mainland municipalities publish their requirements without publishing a review window.

The one exception in our service area is Surrey, which publishes both targets and running averages: **3 business days for a Minor Tenant Improvement, 3 weeks for a Medical Clinic Tenant Improvement and 10 weeks for a New Tenant Improvement**. Two caveats come with those figures — the clock starts on a complete and accurate application rather than at submission, and the reported averages are a rolling figure that drifts.

Vancouver, Burnaby, Richmond, Abbotsford and White Rock each publish requirements and a pathway but no review window. Burnaby is worth flagging separately: it runs tenant improvements on either a fast track or a full plan review, and a plan checker decides which. For the full picture see [what permits a commercial renovation needs in BC](/blog/commercial-renovation-permits-bc/).

The practical advice is the same everywhere: a complete first submission is worth more than any amount of follow-up. Richmond states plainly that it will not accept an incomplete commercial application.

## Phase 4 — Construction

Three to four months for a standard community pharmacy, four to six where compounding is in scope. The sequence runs demolition, framing and rough-in, mechanical and electrical, drywall and finishes, then millwork installation and final inspections.

The rough-in stage is where a pharmacy stops resembling a retail fit-out. Dispensary power and data have to land at exact positions set by the millwork drawings, refrigeration needs dedicated circuits, and the security infrastructure — door hardware, cameras, the safe location — is built into the walls rather than added to them. Changing the dispensary layout after rough-in means opening walls that are already closed.

Costs run **$180–$250 per sq ft** turnkey. A 1,500 sq ft pharmacy is therefore roughly $270,000–$375,000 for the tenant improvement. Our [pharmacy build-out cost guide](/blog/pharmacy-build-out-cost-bc/) breaks that down by size and scope.

## Phase 5 — Licensing and the Pre-Opening Inspection

This is the phase that catches owners out, because it is the only one that cannot be compressed by spending more money.

A community pharmacy in BC is licensed by the **College of Pharmacists of BC**, and the process includes an inspection of the completed premises before the pharmacy may operate. There is also **PharmaNet** connectivity to arrange, and federal requirements administered by **Health Canada** where controlled substances are dispensed. Requirements change, so confirm the current process directly with the College at the start of the project rather than relying on how a colleague's build went three years ago.

The scheduling consequence is simple and important: because the College inspects a finished space, the application has to be well advanced before construction ends. Owners who submit after substantial completion routinely add four to eight weeks to a project that was otherwise on time — paying rent on a finished pharmacy that is not allowed to dispense.

## Phase 6 — Fixturing, Inventory and Systems

Two to four weeks. Shelving and fixtures land after the deficiency list is closed, then inventory, then the software and PharmaNet setup and staff training. It is short, but it is not zero, and it is frequently left off the schedule entirely.

## The Four Things That Actually Cause Delay

1. **Dispensary millwork ordered late.** The longest lead item on the job, and the one most often treated as a finishing decision.
2. **The licence application started after construction.** The single most avoidable delay on a pharmacy build.
3. **An incomplete permit application.** In several municipalities this does not slow the review, it stops it — the application is returned rather than queued.
4. **Base-building electrical capacity discovered late.** Refrigeration, dispensing technology and IT add up, and an upgrade at the panel is neither quick nor cheap once construction has started.

## What Can Run in Parallel

Almost everything, if it is planned that way. Licensing runs alongside construction. Millwork fabrication runs alongside mechanical rough-in. Inventory and software procurement run alongside finishing. Staff recruitment runs alongside all of it.

The projects that take fourteen months rather than eight are rarely slower on site. They are the ones that ran each phase in sequence because nobody mapped what could overlap.

## Conclusion

A pharmacy build in BC is 8 to 14 months from lease to licensed dispensary, and only three or four of those months are construction. The schedule is won in design — by freezing the dispensary layout early, drawing and ordering the millwork before permits are issued, and starting the College of Pharmacists of BC application while the walls are still going up.
